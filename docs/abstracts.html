Fabio Bove and Marco Ferrarini

Thoughts on AI in Therapeutic Discovery

Abstract
Artificial intelligence is expanding what is possible in therapeutic discovery. It enables researchers to explore more hypotheses, connect larger bodies of evidence, and move from static representations of disease targets toward a more dynamic understanding of their biological behaviour.

Its greatest potential, however, may extend beyond generating candidates faster. By connecting in silico predictions with well-designed in vitro validation, AI can help prioritize the most informative experiments, identify weak hypotheses earlier, and make each result -positive or negative- useful for the next decision. This creates a compounding learning effect: as evidence accumulates, models improve, scientific understanding deepens, and future experiments become more focused.

Realizing this potential requires high-quality data, traceable reasoning, and experiments designed to challenge rather than simply confirm predictions. These are not constraints on AI, but the foundations that allow it to become a reliable scientific partner. The quality of the knowledge produced ultimately depends on the technologies, information, and people contributing to the process.

This talk reflects on the evolution from pre-AI discovery workflows to classical AI-based prediction and generation, and toward agentic, human-in-the-loop systems. The opportunity is to build hybrid approaches in which AI amplifies human expertise, accelerates learning across computational and experimental work, and continuously improves how therapeutic hypotheses are generated, evaluated, and translated into evidence.

Dr. Esther Wershof

PRiMeFlow: Winning the ARC Virtual Cell Challenge with Generative Modeling

Abstract

Predicting in-silico cellular responses to genetic and small-molecule perturbations is critical for learning structured relationships between interventions, pathways, and cell states. As predictive AI begins to complement expensive high-throughput perturbation screens, modeling challenges persist due to single-cell heterogeneity and complex latent gene dependencies. Here, we present PRiMeFlow, an end-to-end flow-matching approach designed to model perturbation effects directly in gene expression space. Parameterized with a U-Net velocity field, PRiMeFlow accurately captures empirical single-cell expression distributions, avoiding common failure modes such as mode collapse. Benchmarked extensively using the PerturBench framework, our approach validates key architectural choices and demonstrates how generative systems generalize predictive insights across diverse biological contexts. PRiMeFlow served as the core architecture behind our victory in the ARC Institute’s Virtual Cell Challenge, setting a new benchmark for generative perturbation-response modeling and proving the potential of AI to reliably prioritize therapeutic interventions in silico.

Speaker bio

Esther Wershof is a Senior Scientist in the Machine Learning group at Altos Labs, where she architects AI-driven platforms to model cell rejuvenation and reverse disease. Operating at the cutting edge of lab-in-the-loop engineering, she recently co-led the global team that won the $100k ARC Virtual Cell Challenge at NeurIPS 2025 and launched a pioneering open-source benchmarking platform that is setting new standards for perturbation prediction. She holds a PhD from The Francis Crick Institute on emergent collective behavior in tumors.

Dr. Fabian Frohlich

Deep Mechanistic Models Reveal Pathway-Extrinsic Drivers of Mammary MAPK Signalling Heterogeneity

Abstract

Cells sense and respond to their environment through signalling pathways, and the dynamics of these pathways shape cell fate even within genetically identical populations. Two largely separate computational traditions describe this behaviour: mechanistic differential-equation models and representation-learning methods. Mechanistic models encode pathway topology and kinetics but cannot easily represent variation arising outside the modelled pathway. Representation learning, instead, maps genome-wide measurements onto low-dimensional manifolds but offers no mechanistic account of how the resulting cell states execute their functions. Reconciling these views, explaining signalling heterogeneity in a manner that is at once data-driven and mechanistically interpretable, has remained difficult. Here we introduce deep mechanistic models (DMMs), which couple semi-supervised representation learning to an ordinary-differential-equation model of EGFR/MAPK signalling, trained end-to-end so that the learnt representation and mechanistic parametrisation inform each other. Applying DMMs to multiplexed signalling data from 63 breast cancer cell lines, we show that the models generalise to held-out cell lines and attribute most heterogeneity to pathway-extrinsic factors, namely baseline ERBB2 activation and a Ca2+/p38 signalling axis, rather than to variation in core MAPK components. Where the models fail, the discrepancies pinpoint rare signalling-altering mutations and recurrent programmes, including a putative AMPK-BRAF MEK-inhibitor-resistance axis and a cytoskeletal programme. We further find that mechanistic integration of EGFR receptor levels reshapes the learnt representation, rendering a molecular and a systems-level account of the same data equivalent in predictive power. DMMs thus offer a general framework for fusing mechanism with learning, naturally extendable to further modalities such as imaging, and simultaneously turn model failure into a systematic route to discover and evaluate candidate biology.

Speaker bio

He was awarded a fellowship through the Graduate School of Quantitative Biosciences Munich to complete his PhD at the Technical University of Munich in 2018, working with Fabian Theis and Jan Hasenauer at the Institute for Computational Biology at Helmholtz Centre Munich. Dr. Michail Mamalakis

From Genes to Brain Imaging: Interpretable and Adaptable AI for Understanding Brain Disease

Abstract
Understanding brain disease requires integrating molecular changes with macroscopic alterations observed through magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). I present complementary artificial intelligence frameworks for analysing brain MRI and single-cell RNA sequencing data. AlcaMoena, a three-dimensional MRI foundation model pretrained without manual labels on more than 425,000 volumes and derived maps, combines volumetric encoding, latent diffusion, and Graph-Blueprint Pruning to expand across clinical domains while limiting the loss of previously acquired capabilities. Its representations support image synthesis, disease classification, survival modelling, and postoperative prediction. At the molecular level, I integrate graph-based gene embeddings with transformer-based language models to capture temporal gene dynamics and introduce a drift score that measures changes in gene relationships across biological states. Applied to ependymoma, the framework recovered 70 of 89 known disease-associated genes—a 30% improvement over the best individual method—and identified candidate biomarkers with disease-specific progression patterns. Together, these approaches provide scalable and interpretable tools for connecting cellular dynamics with imaging phenotypes and supporting biomarker discovery and clinical prediction

Dr. Larry Melidis

Predicting Nucleic Acid Structural Diversity Across the Tree of Life

Abstract

Sequence-to-structure prediction has transformed our understanding of proteins, yet predicting the structures adopted by short nucleic acids remains challenging because individual sequences can access diverse and dynamic conformational landscapes. Here, we combine high-throughput experiments with machine learning to quantify and predict the structural diversity of G-rich single-stranded DNA, which can fold into alternative structures known as G-quadruplexes. Rather than predicting a single structural state, our model captures sequence-dependent differences in structural diversity and enables their prediction at scale. We apply this framework across the tree of life to investigate how these structural landscapes have evolved. Our analysis reveals signatures consistent with the selection of particular G-quadruplex-forming sequences as regulatory elements, alongside a large-scale redistribution of G-rich sequence content from archaea to warm-blooded vertebrates. Together, these results connect nucleic-acid sequence, structural diversity and evolution, providing a framework for understanding how dynamic nucleic-acid structures emerge and are exploited across genomes.

Speaker bio

Larry Melidis trained in theoretical and medical physics at the Universities of Surrey and Cambridge, including Part III Mathematics, where he focused on functional analysis and sphere-packing problems. He subsequently moved into molecular biophysics at King’s College London and completed his PhD at the University of Birmingham, developing computational approaches to predict and target viral RNA structures at the interface of chemistry, computer science and medicine. Since 2021, he has been part of the Balasubramanian group at the University of Cambridge, where he is now the lead bioinformatician. His research uses computation, machine learning and high-throughput experiments to understand how nucleic-acid sequence encodes structural diversity, and how these alternative structures are exploited in transcription and disease.

Dr. Anurag Limdi

Uncertainty Quantification in Multi-Omic Generative Models Identifies Context-Dependent Biology and Technical Noise

Abstract

Uncertainty quantification is becoming essential to AI/ML models as their predictions increasingly guide real-world decisions, and multi-omic biology is a compelling example. Generative models are increasingly used to impute missing molecular measurements, and their predictions often feed directly into downstream analyses like biomarker discovery and target prioritization, usually without any indication of which predictions can be trusted. We introduce MOCHA, a framework that equips multi-omic generative models with calibrated, prediction-level uncertainty. Applied to seven modalities across 1,523 cancer cell lines from the Cancer Dependency Map, MOCHA’s uncertainty estimates are well calibrated and, when normalized by feature variability, yield a “relative uncertainty” score quantifying how much of a feature’s variation is explainable from molecular context. Low relative uncertainty recovers context-dependent cancer vulnerabilities and known synthetic lethal interactions; high relative uncertainty blindly detects a known quality difference between two drug-screening campaigns. Together, these results extend uncertainty quantification from a purely model-centric diagnostic into a systems-level readout of how information is distributed across molecular layers, separating what is learnable biology from what is not.

Speaker bio

Anurag Limdi was most recently a Machine Learning Scientist at EMBL-EBI / Open Targets as part of the Digital Twins for Rare Diseases project, where his work focused on uncertainty quantification in generative models of multi-omic data. Previously, he was at Apriori Bio, a Boston-based startup, designing variant-proof vaccines for viral outbreaks through sequence-to-function modelling, protein library design, and statistical analysis of deep mutational scanning experiments. He completed his PhD at Harvard University in Michael Baym’s group, where he explored mutational robustness and predictability of evolutionary trajectories in microbial populations. Broadly, his interests span protein design, molecular evolution, human genomics, and applying machine learning to problems in biology. Trained as a systems biologist at the intersection of computational and lab science, he believes in deeply understanding the quirks and biases in biological datasets in order to build generalizable models.

Prof. Tom McClelland

The Problem of Artificial Consciousness

Abstract
The notion of artificial consciousness is now gaining significant traction with developers, users and researchers. How seriously should we take this idea? When should we conclude that an AI is conscious? And what are the ethical ramifications of creating conscious AI? I argue that it is very difficult to determine the prospects of consciousness scientifically and suggest how to navigate this uncertainty responsibly.

Speaker bio
Tom McClelland is a Lecturer in the Department of History and Philosophy of Science at the University of Cambridge and an Associate Fellow of the Leverhulme Centre for the Future of Intelligence. His work in AI explores whether AI has the potential to be conscious and/or creative.

Dr. Johann Hawe

AI-Powered Interpretation of Human Genetic Variation

Abstract

The vast majority of human genetic variants remain poorly understood, limiting our ability to diagnose disease and identify new therapeutic opportunities. The Illumina Artificial Intelligence Lab develops deep learning models that leverage genomic sequence together with large-scale functional genomics data to predict the consequences of human genetic variation. In this presentation, I will highlight recent advances in AI-driven variant interpretation, including SpliceAI for predicting splicing defects, PrimateAI-3D for coding variant pathogenicity prediction, and PromoterAI for understanding the effects of regulatory variants. These approaches improve interpretation of both coding and non-coding variation, enable discovery of disease-associated genes and pathways, and support the identification of novel therapeutic targets. Together, these advances illustrate how AI can help bridge the gap between genomic variation and clinical impact.

Speaker bio

Johann Hawe is a Senior Staff Bioinformatics Scientist in the Illumina AI Lab, where he develops and applies deep learning methods for genomic variant interpretation. His work focuses on understanding the functional impact of coding and non-coding genetic variation to advance rare disease diagnosis, precision medicine, and therapeutic target discovery.

Dr. Ilaria Billato

Beyond the Slide: Integrating Histopathological Image Features with Multi-Omics Data in R/Bioconductor

Abstract

Histopathological images provide unique insight into tissue architecture, cellular morphology, and tumor spatial organization, yet their computational analysis largely happens outside the R/Bioconductor ecosystem, the primary environment for omics analysis, creating a barrier to truly integrative multi-modal studies. We address this gap with a suite of R/Bioconductor packages built around a large-scale, pre-computed image feature resource derived from the complete TCGA collection of diagnostic H&E whole-slide images (11,765 images, 9,640 cases, 32 cancer types). imageFeatureTCGA provides the pre-computed nuclear segmentation and classification (via HoVer-Net) and tile-/slide-level embeddings (via the ProvGigaPath foundation model), organized in Bioconductor-native structures (SpatialExperiment, SpatialFeatureExperiment, MultiAssayExperiment); imageTCGAutils handles data retrieval and matching of image features to TCGA identifiers; HistoImagePlot offers spatial and compositional visualization; and imageTCGA provides an exploratory interface linking features to the original images. These resources make histopathological features directly integrable with TCGA molecular data, RNA-seq, methylation, mutations, copy-number alterations, and clinical annotations. We illustrate the framework with two case studies: an ovarian cancer analysis, and a TCGA-BRCA case study integrating ProvGigaPath slide embeddings with RNA-seq, RPPA, miRNA and CNV data via MOFA+, where latent factors capture the proliferative/Basal-like versus luminal axis and several are significantly associated with overall survival. Together, these tools bridge histopathological image analysis and multi-omic integration within a unified R/Bioconductor framework, supporting hypothesis generation and validation across tumor types.

Speaker bio

Ilaria Billato is a postdoctoral researcher in the Department of Biology at the University of Padova, in the Romualdi Lab, with a background in statistics. She works at the interface of computational pathology, bioinformatics, and multi-omic data integration, spanning histopathology image analysis, spatial transcriptomics, and clinical-omics studies, with a focus on foundation models applied to biomedical imaging. She is an active member of the Bioconductor “Multi-omic Integration of Histopathology Image Analysis” working group.

Dr. Bianca Pierattini

Structure at Scale: Turning RNA into Training Data for Predictive Models

Abstract

RNA structure is a central regulator of gene expression, yet our ability to predict how sequence dictates folding and how folding shapes function remains far behind equivalent advances in protein structure prediction. A major bottleneck is the lack of large-scale, high-confidence experimental datasets linking RNA sequence, structure, and function within the native cellular environment.

This project uses a synthetic RNA platform to address this gap. A library of plasmid-encoded RNA sequences spanning major structural motif families will be introduced into mammalian cells and analysed using two orthogonal in-cell structural probing methods, COMRADES and SHAPE-MaP, alongside functional reporter assays measuring RNA stability and translation. Together, these will generate the first large-scale, quantitative map of RNA sequence–structure–function relationships in cells.

By generating high-throughput experimental data of the kind that has been unavailable for RNA, this platform aims to provide the training resource needed for the next generation of RNA structure-prediction models, the kind of systematic, high-quality dataset that underpinned recent breakthroughs in protein structure prediction. This talk will present the platform, preliminary data, and the broader opportunity for the ML community to build predictive, generative models of RNA structure once such data becomes available.

Speaker bio

Bianca Pierattini is a Herchel Smith Research Fellow in the Department of Biochemistry at the University of Cambridge, working with Professor Eric Miska. From 2027, she will continue her research as a BBSRC Fellow. Her work develops high-throughput, in-cell platforms to map RNA structure and function, using libraries of synthetic RNA sequences combined with orthogonal structural probing and functional readouts. Building on these large-scale structural datasets, she is beginning to explore how machine learning could be used to model and predict RNA sequence–structure–function relationships, with a view to addressing data bottlenecks in RNA biology and supporting future therapeutic discovery.

Bianca completed her PhD in Functional and Structural Genomics at SISSA, Trieste, under Professor Stefano Gustincich, where her work showed that N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification regulates the activity of SINEUP long non-coding RNAs. She co-organises the Cambridge RNA Club and is actively involved in initiatives promoting collaboration and scientific exchange within the wider RNA research community.

Dr. Tania Bobbo

Machine Learning for Probiotics Discovery: The PROB-AI Project

Abstract
Probiotics are microorganisms that confer health benefits to humans. Most known probiotics belong to the Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Bacillus, Streptococcus, and Enterococcus genera, although new probiotic taxa continue to emerge. To be considered probiotic, bacterial strains must be safe for human use, survive passage through the gastrointestinal tract, and colonize the gut. These properties are typically assessed through extensive in vitro and in vivo experiments, making probiotic discovery slow and costly. The PROB-AI project aims to accelerate this process by developing machine-learning methods to identify probiotic bacteria directly from genomic data. ML models trained on confirmed probiotic genomes have been used to predict novel candidate strains, providing insights into the genomic determinants of probiotic traits and supporting the discovery of new microorganisms for biomedical and pharmaceutical applications. This ML-based approach represents an innovative and still underexplored strategy with significant potential for microbiology, industry, and human health. We analysed a dataset of approximately 1,000 bacterial genomes, including around 30 confirmed probiotic strains from public repositories. Genomic features were extracted from NCBI genome sequences using the GRRAP in-house developed tool, together with k-mers. Different ML models were fine-tuned using repeated k-fold cross-validation. The best-performing models were retrained on the training set and evaluated on a held-out subset. Finally, the best models were applied to an independent dataset of approximately 1,500 unlabelled bacterial genomes from NCBI to identify novel candidate probiotic strains to be experimentally validated in vitro.

Speaker bio
Tania Bobbo is a researcher at the National Research Council, CNR, Italy, with expertise in applying biostatistics, bioinformatics, and machine learning to the analysis of plant, animal, and human phenotypic, genetic, and genomic data. Her current research focuses on the development and application of AI algorithms for the analysis of bacterial genome sequences to identify novel probiotic strains.

Constrained Generation of Molecular Structures

Abstract

Molecular generation is a fundamental task in computational chemistry, with important applications in drug discovery and materials design. A key challenge is producing molecules that are both chemically valid and structurally plausible, including appropriate ring systems and realistic three-dimensional spatial arrangements. Although recent generative models have demonstrated strong performance, the incorporation of structural constraints remains an open problem.

Flavio Sartori

Scoring Tissue Health from Microscopy to Enable Causal Analysis in Dry AMD

Abstract
Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a common cause of central vision loss, and its dry form still has no treatment for advanced stages. A key difficulty is distinguishing a genuine drug effect on the retinal pigment epithelium from technical noise. We address this with a health score derived directly from microscopy images. After cleaning and normalising the data, each tissue region is scored according to its deviation from the healthy control state. This score then becomes one of the per-patch variables used in a causal model, alongside the applied compound and other local measurements, helping disentangle how much of the observed change is actually caused by each compound in dry AMD.

Matteo Baldan

Learning to Simulate: Neural Operators and the Generation of Physical Dynamics with Applications in Cardiac Electrophysiology

Abstract
Many physical and biological processes can be represented as dynamical systems whose internal state evolves in response to initial conditions, external inputs, and system parameters. Mechanistic simulators approximate this evolution by combining mathematical models with numerical discretisation. Although these approaches remain essential for scientific understanding, their computational cost can limit parameter exploration, uncertainty quantification and the simulation of complex, subject-specific systems. Neural operators provide an emerging alternative: instead of learning predictions at a fixed number of coordinates, they learn mappings between functions and can therefore approximate the evolution or solution operator of an entire family of dynamical systems.

This talk introduces neural operators from an engineering and state-space perspective. After briefly tracing their development from classical operator theory and data-driven system identification to modern deep operator networks, Fourier neural operators and geometry-aware architectures, we describe their mathematical foundations through the concepts of state, input, observation and evolution operators. In this formulation, a neural operator may learn either the map from system conditions to a complete trajectory or the transition operator that advances the state through time.

Particular attention is given to how a continuous operator becomes a computational architecture. Its kernel integration must ultimately be approximated over grids, meshes or graphs, requiring aggregation rules that are independent of node ordering and robust to changes in spatial discretisation. The choice of kernel variables—such as physical distance, relative position, direction and material properties—therefore provides an important engineering inductive bias linking the learned architecture to the dynamics of the system.

Examples from biological modelling illustrate how operator learning can represent multiscale systems whose states are spatially distributed and only partially observed. Cardiac electrophysiology is then presented as a central application: electrical activation emerges from nonlinear reaction-diffusion dynamics on heterogeneous, anisotropic and patient-specific cardiac geometries. We discuss the basics of electrocardiophysiology and of learning surrogate models of transmembrane potential regression

Andrea G. Di Francesco

RAG Beyond NLP: Learning Relevant Context for Cellular Prediction

Abstract
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is usually presented as a paradigm for language models: retrieve relevant documents using a fixed similarity function, then condition generation on them. Outside NLP, however, relevance may be neither obvious nor context-independent.

I will present PT-RAG, a differentiable retrieval framework for predicting single-cell responses to genetic perturbations. PT-RAG first retrieves candidate perturbations using semantic gene representations, then learns which candidates are useful for a particular query and cellular state through an end-to-end differentiable selector. A central empirical finding is that naïve retrieval can actively degrade prediction, whereas learned, cell-state-aware selection improves reconstruction and the fidelity of predicted cell-population distributions across different generative backbones.

The broader message is that extending RAG beyond language is not simply a matter of attaching a vector database to a new model. It requires us to reconsider what the retrievable units should be, how information should be represented, and whether and how relevance itself must be learned from the downstream objective. I will discuss how cellular perturbation prediction exposes these challenges and outline opportunities for neural retrieval in scientific domains, including context-conditioned retrieval and possible directions in richer multimodal representations

Salvatore Romano

Geometric Deep Learning Application in Biology: Two Case Studies.

Abstract

Geometric Deep Learning provides a natural framework for representing the complex relational structure of biological systems. In this work, we present two applications of graph-based deep learning to biological prediction and discovery. First, we develop a descriptor-free Graph Neural Network for antibacterial screening against Acinetobacter baumannii, using only molecular graph information to enable direct interpretation of model predictions. Atom-level attribution identifies a robust substructural motif associated with predicted activity, and prospective screening followed by computational triage identifies candidates for experimental validation, with a selected compound showing dose-dependent growth inhibition in vitro. Second, we investigate immunogenicity prediction of peptide–MHC (pMHC) complexes using heterogeneous Graph Neural Networks combined with structural information generated by AlphaFold-Multimer. By integrating heterogeneous molecular and structural relationships, the approach aims to capture interactions relevant to immune recognition and improve prediction of pMHC immunogenicity. Together, these case studies illustrate how Geometric Deep Learning can leverage biological structure and relational information for both interpretable molecular discovery and immune-response prediction.

Speaker bio

Salvatore Romano is a PhD student enrolled in the National PhD in Artificial Intelligence, XXXIX cycle, organized by University Campus Bio-Medico of Rome (Rome, Italy) andaffiliated with University of Catania (Catania, Italy). His research interests span from geometric deep learning to graph generation (guidance and evaluation) and their application to the biological domain.
